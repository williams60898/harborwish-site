## Your Six Dimensions

### ① Valence: Cold

The base color of your feeling runs cold. Not just-today cold — it’s been cold for a while now.

### ② Energy: Low

You look calm — but it isn’t calm, it’s permafrost. Nothing moves underneath, because everything underneath is frozen.

### ③ Agency: In Control

You weren’t knocked down. You closed the door yourself, turned, and climbed. That step was yours.

### ④ Social: Avoidant

You’re stepping back, away from people — and you know you’re doing it. It’s deliberate.

### ⑤ Time: Past

Your gaze keeps drifting back, on its own, to what has already happened, already ended.

### ⑥ Clarity: Clear

You know exactly what you’re feeling, and exactly why. Not the least bit foggy.

## Your Portrait

On the day it happened, you didn’t fall apart. You didn’t scream, didn’t post a vague line of a song late at night, didn’t dial anyone’s number at two in the morning. You did something else — much quieter, and honestly, much more frightening: you picked up every shattered piece off the floor, laid them out under a hard white light, went over them one by one, and assembled in your mind a complete case file, precise as a coroner’s report.

Which day the crack started. Whose grip loosened first. Where you took the wrong step. How that one thing they said had been a signal all along. Which supposedly unbreakable moment the fault line first appeared in — about what actually happened, you understand it more clearly, more coldly, and closer to the truth than anyone who was there.

There’s just one problem: understanding all of it didn’t make anything better. It only made you a little quieter.

It’s not that you can’t say it, either. It’s that you’ve already rehearsed every version of the conversation in your head — the vulnerable version, the furious version, the calm-and-reasonable version — and every time you run it, the ending is the same: saying it changes nothing.

So you made the only decision that seemed logical to you: you went up the mountain. Now you’re above the snowline. The air is thin, the view astonishing. You can see every light in the valley below, can hear the faint laughter carried up on the wind. You see all of it, perfectly clearly.

You just — don’t go back down.

> Your clarity was never meant to be a wall. It was meant to be a lamp — not to keep people out, but to light your way back to them.

## If This Page Cuts Too Deep

There’s one thing that has to be said to you — gently, and in earnest.

The snow leopard’s danger hides precisely in its composure. You look so much like someone who is “fine now” — calm, self-possessed, having thought it all through — that you may have fooled even yourself. So, for your own sake, watch for one moment: when “I see through all of it” quietly begins to turn into “none of it means anything anyway,” “it’s fine not to go back down,” “there’s nothing worth staying for” — that is very likely not clarity, but a quieter kind of giving up.

For the snow leopard, the thing to watch was never how loud the pain gets, but the signal going dark: needing less and less effort, needing no one, caring about less and less. This kind of “calm” needs to be seen by someone more than any breakdown ever would.

If you recognize this moment, remember what Harborwish says from the start: it is a mirror, not a doctor. It isn’t enough. Go find the person who can catch you — a professional counselor or doctor, or that one person in your life you trust, the one you’d be willing to tell the truth. Take all of your clarity, and go to them. This isn’t weakness. It’s the most clear-eyed thing you, right now, can do for yourself.

## The Animals Nearest You

On this map, the two animals closest to you differ from you by a single direction each — and it’s that one direction that decides who you are.

The Ice-Keeping Polar Bear differs from you in only the last dimension: you are Clear, it is Blurred. The two of you are equally cold, equally withdrawn, equally given to looking back over your shoulder again and again. But the polar bear’s looking-back is out of focus — a vague, nameless heaviness sits on it; it knows it hurts but can’t say where, like watching itself through frosted glass. You are the opposite: your pain is in focus, sharp enough to file, to label, to recite back word for word. It is trapped in “I can’t put it into words”; you are trapped in “I understand it too well.”

The Roaming Gray Wolf differs from you in the single dimension of time: you look back at what has already happened; it stares hard at what is about to come. The two of you are equally clear-eyed, equally on guard, equally alone in it. But the wolf’s eyes are fixed ahead — wary of the next blow, weighing the next move, guarding against a threat that hasn’t shown itself yet. Your eyes are fixed behind — on things long over, things that can never be changed. The wolf goes taut for what is coming; you lock yourself down for what is already gone.

Recognize this, and you understand: you are not a lone snow leopard — you are a coordinate in this cold country. Shift one square to the side, and there’s another way to live. And what Harborwish wants to help you see is exactly this: from here, where you could still go.
