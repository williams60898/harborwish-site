## Your Six Dimensions

### ① Valence: Cold

Your base color is fear. Those spines aren’t ferocity, they’re dread — you’re wound tight at all times, because to your eyes the world is full of things that could lunge at you out of nowhere.

### ② Energy: High

The instant you’re spooked, you blow up — whole body on guard, every spine erect. That surge comes on hard and burns fierce; you often spend an entire day “puffed,” worn to collapse.

### ③ Agency: In Control

This is the bit of footing you haven’t lost. When frightened, your instinct isn’t to freeze, or flee, or shrink into a ball for the taking — it’s to make yourself big, to let them know: *don’t come near*. You didn’t hand yourself over as prey.

### ④ Social: Avoidant

The whole of you faces *don’t touch me*. Spines out, blown into a ball no hand will reach for — you think it’s protection; it’s actually keeping everyone outside the door, including the ones who came to hold you.

### ⑤ Time: Future

Your spines are always up for the next one. Not resentment of who hurt you before — a fixed stare at “what might lunge next,” so you can’t unclench for even a moment.

### ⑥ Clarity: Blurred

Your deepest pain is here: you can’t tell the difference. A gust of wind, a passing shadow, a well-meaning approach — to your eyes they look identical to real danger, so you blow up at everything. It’s not that you don’t want to see clearly. You’re too scared — scared to the point of puffing into spines before you can even look.

## Your Portrait

When you’re not startled, you’re actually round, soft, unhurried — a little clumsily endearing, even — bobbing along, looking harmless. But that’s only the surface. You’re always ready to blow. One rustle of grass, and in a fraction of a second you’ve inflated into a ball of hard spines, every one pointed out: *reach for me and you’ll regret it.*

The part that leaves everyone helplessly stuck — and mortifies you most — is that the thing that scared you often isn’t dangerous at all. A slightly stronger current, a shadow drifting by, a hand that only meant to pet you — you blow up in an earth-shaking panic, and only afterward find it was a false alarm. You’re not putting on airs; in that instant, you genuinely thought you were done for. That’s the most exhausting part: other people startle, take two seconds, and it’s over — you have to blow up, stay bristled for hours, heart in your throat, before you can slowly confirm, *oh... nothing again.*

And there’s a deeper fear underneath. You’re not only afraid of being hurt — you’re also afraid that once you panic, you’ll hurt the kind person who came close. So you pull the distance wider: half of it is fear of being touched, and the other half, fear of accidentally stabbing someone. It isn’t that you don’t want closeness — it’s that even closeness itself costs you double.

The most tiring part is that you can’t tell them apart. Real danger and harmless approach look the same to you — both make you blow. You can’t look first and then decide whether to bristle; your order is always reversed: blow first, then (maybe) discover, *oh, it was nothing.* By then the person’s long since been stabbed away. So day after day you stay puffed, stay tensed, spending yourself dry for threats that mostly don’t exist. Only deep in the night, when it’s truly empty, do you dare deflate — inch by inch — back into that round, soft self, too tired to even be afraid. And the next day, another shadow drifts by, and you blow into a ball of spines again.

> Those spines of yours — the one they stab deepest was never anyone else. It’s the you trapped inside them.

## If This Page Cuts Too Deep

There’s one thing that has to be said to you — gently, and in earnest.

The pufferfish’s danger hides behind how ordinary it looks — you blow up so often that everyone, you included, learns to read it as just another false alarm, nothing worth watching. So, for your own sake, watch for one turn — and for the pufferfish especially, watch for the puffing stopping. When you slide from *spooked into spines* toward *puffed so long you can never deflate* — an unnatural exhaustion, a numb detachment, a flat *whatever, none of it matters* calm — or when the spines turn inward, into “I’m a burden to everyone” and “everyone’s better off without me” — or when a creature that always went off at a touch suddenly stops going off, unnaturally quiet, unnaturally agreeable, starting to arrange things, to say goodbye — that is very likely not the fear finally easing, but a quieter kind of giving up.

For the pufferfish, what to watch for was never how loud the pain gets — it’s the signal going quiet: needing less, reaching for no one, caring about less and less. That kind of calm needs to be seen by someone, more than any breakdown ever would. While the spines are still up, you’re still fighting; it’s when the spines vanish all at once and you go strangely still that it matters most.

If you recognize this turn, remember what Harborwish says from the start: it is a mirror, not a doctor. It isn’t enough. Go find the person who can catch you — a counselor or doctor, or the one person you trust enough to tell the truth. This isn’t weakness. It’s the most clear-eyed thing you can do for yourself right now.

## The Animals Nearest You

On this map, the two animals closest to you each differ from you by just one dimension — and that one dimension decides which kind of defense you are.

The Bristling Hedgehog differs from you in just one dimension: agency. You’re both defenders who throw up something sharp the moment you’re startled, both holding the world off with a coat of spikes. The only difference is direction. The hedgehog curls inward — balls up, makes itself small, *I just want to hide, leave me alone* (carried along). You blow outward — inflate, make yourself big, *back off, you’ll get stabbed* (in control). Same coat of spines: it draws inward and hides itself away; you push outward and plant yourself in place. It chooses to disappear; you choose to hold your ground.

The Warning Rattlesnake differs from you in just one dimension: clarity. You’re both the type to show the defensive pose first. But the rattlesnake is clear-eyed — it rattles a warning first, knows what it’s doing, knows whether the threat is real, and strikes only if it must. You don’t. You can’t tell. The rattlesnake’s pose says *I warned you*; your spines say *I blew up before I could think*. Its guard is aimed and precise; yours is set off by a gust of wind, spraying in every direction at once.
