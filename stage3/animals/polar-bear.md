## Your Six Dimensions

### ① Valence: Cold

Your base color is loss — the things you loved, leaned on, grew used to are pulling back from under your feet, one by one.

### ② Energy: Low

You don’t wail or thrash. You just slow down — slow as if rationing the last of your strength for this whole frozen waste.

### ③ Agency: In Control

You were the one who called it on this ice, used to carrying it alone, holding it up alone — but you don’t dare count how much you have left to hold, and can’t say when it became only you, still standing.

### ④ Social: Avoidant

You’re a solitary animal by nature, alone for most of your life — not that you can’t get close, but you tucked getting close away inside your shell long ago.

### ⑤ Time: Past

You’re always looking back — at the ice that hasn’t melted yet, the person still beside you, the old world that hadn’t collapsed.

### ⑥ Clarity: Blurred

You live inside a whiteout: how long the ice will hold, when the warmth will come, who exactly you’re waiting for — all of it blown into one white blur. The strongest bear, caught in a whiteout, goes snow-blind too: can’t find its direction, can’t make out even itself.

## Your Portrait

You are the kind who stands at the highest point and carries not a trace of arrogance.

People look at you and assume you’re strong: the size, the territory, the ease of going it alone — the very picture of a king who needs no one. Only you know how much *don’t dare need* is hidden inside your *don’t need*. You can carry very heavy things, so everyone assumes you can carry anything — including the things that are quietly crushing you. The ice under your feet is melting, inch by inch, and no one sees it. You pretend not to see it either.

Your strength was never for attack. It’s for holding — holding up a situation already caving in, a bond already gone cold, that thin hope of *maybe if I just wait a little longer, it’ll be all right*. You’re slow not from laziness but from rationing: you know too well that strength runs out, that no resupply is coming across this waste, so you save every ounce — save it until it looks like numbness. And all around is the white of the whiteout, the wind flattening every direction — until you can no longer tell whether you’re waiting for someone to come back, or just afraid to stop.

The softest part of you, you never show anyone. It’s frozen at the very bottom, so deep you’ve half-forgotten it can still ache.

> The ice was never the love. The ones who leaned on you here — they were. Before it all melts, go hold the ones still standing.

## If This Page Cuts Too Deep

There’s one thing that has to be said to you — gently, and in earnest.

The polar bear’s danger sinks so slowly it’s easy to mistake for *he’s just cold, just quiet*. You carry everything without a sound, and the quieter you go, the more it needs watching. So, for your own sake, watch for one turn: when “I can carry it” slides toward “I can’t carry it anymore, and there’s no point carrying it” — when “I couldn’t keep them” and “everyone leaves” and “I’m meant to be alone” stop sounding like grief and settle into a flat, calm certainty — when the words *melting*, *gone*, *disappeared* stop pointing at the ice and start pointing at you — that is very likely not thinking-it-through, but a quieter kind of giving up.

For the polar bear, what to watch for was never how loud the pain gets — it’s the signal going quiet: needing less, reaching for no one, caring about less and less, until even wanting is gone. That kind of calm needs to be seen by someone, more than any breakdown ever would. The world melting does not mean you have to melt with it.

If you recognize this turn, remember what Harborwish says from the start: it is a mirror, not a doctor. It isn’t enough. Go find the person who can catch you — a counselor or doctor, or the one person you trust enough to tell the truth. This isn’t weakness. It’s the most clear-eyed thing you can do for yourself right now.

## The Animals Nearest You

On this map, the animals closest to you each differ from you by a dimension or two — and a dimension or two is another whole way to live.

The Gazing Snow Leopard stands one dimension from you: it is Clear, you are Blurred. You both stand high, both go alone, both look down at the world below. But the snow leopard’s solitude is a clear-eyed choice — it knows exactly why it doesn’t come close; that distance was worked out and decided. Yours is a blurred endurance: you didn’t choose solitude — the ice melted floe by floe, the ones who leaned on you walked off one by one, and you’re left standing, unable to see how you ended up alone. The snow leopard says *I decided to stand here by myself.* You say *I woke to find myself the only one still standing.* Both are the cold of high, thin air — but one is a cold held in the fist, and the other a cold that leaked in through the cracks.

The Roaming Gray Wolf differs from you by two dimensions — where it looks, and how clearly. The wolf also goes alone, also holds the reins, but the wolf faces forward and sees clearly; it picks its road and walks, and solitude is the price of moving on. You do the reverse: you keep looking back, and can’t see the road ahead. You don’t run forward — you hold your post, guarding the ice not yet melted, aching to return to when it was thick and everyone was still here.

The Deep-Diving Humpback stands one dimension away: how you face people. You both look back, both can’t see what’s coming, both carry it alone. But the humpback sings out to find its own kind — even if no one understands, it makes a sound. You do the opposite: when it hurts, your first move is to pull back into the ice and swallow the sound, as if even asking for help were too loud.

The Sinking Manatee stands one dimension from you: who holds the reins. You both drift to the water’s edge, both look back, both can’t see ahead. But the manatee is held by the water and sinks, inch by inch — not that it doesn’t want to float, but the very strength and will to try have loosened their grip. You do the reverse: you insist on holding yourself up, refusing to sink, jaw set, standing even as the ice dissolves beneath you. The manatee sinks its haze into *I’ve stopped moving*; you brace your haze into *I can’t fall*.

Recognize this, and you understand: you’re not a polar bear doomed to the last floe — you’re a point on a cold map. Step one square over, and there’s another way to live. And that is exactly what Harborwish wants to help you see: from here, where you could still go.
